\subsection*{System Overview}
The proposed system consists of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Purified water tank
    \item Concentrated chemical supply tank
    \item SS316 mixing chamber tank with 10L capacity
    \item Top-mounted agitator system
    \item Distribution diaphragm pump
    \item Common dispensing pipeline
    \item Dispensing tanks
    \item ESP32-based controller
\end{itemize}

The peristaltic pump is used only for accurate concentrated chemical dosing into the 10L SS316 stainless steel mixing chamber. Purified water and concentrated chemical enter the mixing chamber, where a top-mounted DC agitator motor with a mixing blade continuously mixes the solution before distribution. After the chemical is uniformly mixed with purified water, a separate 12V DC diaphragm pump transfers the mixed solution from the mixing chamber into the common dispensing pipeline. This pipeline then distributes the prepared solution to multiple dispensing tanks located in different hospital areas.


\chapter{Method to Measure Water Level and Remaining Chemical Levels}

\section{Water Level Measurement and Chemical Estimation}

\subsection{Water Tank Measurement}

A waterproof ultrasonic sensor was selected for measuring the purified water tank level because the hospital requested that no sensing device should be installed inside the tank due to periodic cleaning and maintenance operations.

The ultrasonic sensor is mounted at the top of the tank and measures the distance between the sensor and the water surface. Since the sensor measures the empty space above the water, the actual water level can be determined by subtracting the measured distance from the effective internal height of the tank.

To improve measurement accuracy, the thickness of the tank lid and the wall thickness of the cylindrical tank are considered during calibration. The internal dimensions of the tank are used instead of external dimensions when calculating the actual water capacity.

The tank dimensions, wall thickness, lid thickness, and chemical density values are pre-determined during system installation and calibration. These calibrated parameters are stored in the ESP32 controller and are used in subsequent level and volume calculations during system operation.

\noindent Let:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $LT$ = Lid thickness
    \item $WT$ = Tank wall thickness
    \item $H_{ext}$ = External tank height
    \item $D_{ext}$ = External tank diameter
    \item $d$ = Measured distance from ultrasonic sensor to water surface
\end{itemize}

The effective internal tank height is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
H_{int} = H_{ext} - LT - WT
\end{equation}

The effective internal tank radius is:

\begin{equation}
r_{int} = \frac{D_{ext}}{2} - WT
\end{equation}

The total internal tank volume is:

\begin{equation}
V_{tank} = \pi r_{int}^{2} H_{int}
\end{equation}

The current water level inside the tank is calculated using:

\begin{equation}
Water\ Level = H_{int} - d
\end{equation}

The remaining water volume is estimated as:

\begin{equation}
V_{water} = \pi r_{int}^{2} (Water\ Level)
\end{equation}

This method provides:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Non-contact operation
    \item Hygienic measurement
    \item Low maintenance
    \item Protection against sensor damage during tank cleaning
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Chemical Estimation and Dispensing Control}

The system contains two separate liquid systems:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Concentrated chemical storage tank
    \item Diluted chemical dispensing tanks
\end{itemize}

\noindent Each dispensing tank uses a capacitive sensor to detect low-level conditions and generate refill requests to the ESP32 controller.

\noindent The concentrated chemical tank uses two monitoring methods:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Hall-effect flow sensor
    \item Load cell weight sensor
\end{itemize}

The flow sensor continuously measures the amount of concentrated chemical dispensed into the mixing chamber. The ESP32 controller maintains a cumulative record of the total dispensed chemical volume.

\noindent The remaining chemical quantity can be estimated using:

\begin{equation}
Remaining\ Chemical = Initial\ Volume - \sum Dispensed\ Volume
\end{equation}

A load cell placed beneath the concentrated chemical tank continuously measures the tank weight. The measured weight reduction is compared against the expected dispensed chemical amount obtained from the flow sensor.

\noindent The relationship between weight and volume is:

\begin{equation}
Mass = Density \times Volume
\end{equation}

The density of the concentrated chemical is assumed to be known beforehand based on manufacturer specifications or laboratory measurements and is stored in the controller during system calibration.

Therefore:

\begin{equation}
Volume = \frac{Mass}{Density}
\end{equation}

This allows the system to estimate the remaining chemical quantity using both flow-based calculation and weight-based verification.

\noindent The difference between expected and measured chemical reduction can be calculated as:

\begin{equation}
Error = |Expected\ Reduction - Measured\ Reduction|
\end{equation}

If the error exceeds a predefined threshold, the system assumes abnormal operation such as:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Pipeline leakage
    \item Pump malfunction
    \item Flow sensor failure
    \item Unexpected chemical loss
\end{itemize}

The concentrated chemical dosing process is controlled using a PWM-controlled peristaltic pump together with a flow sensor. The peristaltic pump is used for accurate dosing into the 10L SS316 mixing chamber because the liquid only contacts the tube and the flow can be measured directly. Although PWM adjusts the pump speed, it does not guarantee accurate liquid flow due to pressure variations, tubing resistance, viscosity changes, and pump wear.
Therefore, actual flow measurement is required for accurate dispensing control.

Inside the mixing chamber, a top-mounted DC agitator motor rotates a mixing blade to maintain continuous mixing. This helps maintain uniform chemical concentration before the solution is supplied to the distribution line.

After mixing, a 12V DC diaphragm pump is placed after the mixing chamber and before the common dispensing pipeline. This pump is not used for concentration control; its purpose is to distribute the mixed solution from the mixing chamber to multiple dispensing tanks with stable pressure.

The refill operation only begins after all safety conditions are validated by the ESP32 controller.
The required pre-conditions are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Sufficient water available
    \item Sufficient concentrated chemical available
    \item No leakage detected
    \item No sensor faults detected
    \item No overflow condition detected
\end{itemize}

This safety validation process acts as an interlock mechanism to prevent unsafe dispensing conditions.

The refill operation stops automatically once the required target volume has been measured by the flow sensor, mixed in the agitator-equipped mixing chamber, and delivered through the distribution pump and common dispensing pipeline.

\section{Monitoring and Detection Methods}

The proposed automated chemical dispensing system uses both sensor-based and computational methods for monitoring system operation and detecting abnormal conditions.

\subsection{Sensor-Based Monitoring Methods}

\begin{itemize}

    \item Ultrasonic sensor for purified water tank level monitoring

    \item Capacitive sensor for dispensing tank low-level detection

    \item Hall-effect flow sensor for measuring liquid flow rate and dispensed volume

    \item Load cell sensor for monitoring concentrated chemical quantity

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Computational and Algorithmic Methods}

\begin{itemize}

    \item Remaining chemical estimation using cumulative flow measurements

    \item Detection of abnormal flow conditions using flow pulse monitoring

    \item Refill timeout monitoring for detecting pump or valve failures

    \item Agitator operation monitoring during the mixing stage

    \item Leakage detection by comparing expected and measured chemical reduction

    \item Sequential refill scheduling algorithm for managing multiple dispensing tanks

\end{itemize}

\section{Fail-Safe Strategy}

The system is designed with multiple fail-safe mechanisms to reduce operational risks in hospital environments. Automatic shutdown procedures are used during abnormal conditions such as empty tanks, pump failures, leakage detection, or communication failures. Audible alarms and warning notifications are also generated to alert maintenance personnel.

Normally closed solenoid valves are used to automatically stop liquid flow during power failures, reducing the risk of leakage or uncontrolled dispensing. The use of both sensor-based monitoring and computational estimation improves overall system reliability and fault detection capability.

\section{Data Collection from Dispensing Tanks}

The dispensing tanks are distributed throughout different locations in the hospital. Each dispensing tank contains sensors for monitoring liquid levels and refill requests. The sensor data from these tanks must be transmitted to a central controller for monitoring, refill management, alarm handling, and report generation.

Each dispensing tank can use a local ESP32 controller to collect sensor data such as:

\begin{itemize}

    \item Dispensing tank level status

    \item Refill requests

    \item Flow sensor readings

    \item Fault conditions

    \item System status information

\end{itemize}

The collected data is then communicated to a central controller or monitoring server through a wireless communication network.

\section{Selected Communication Method}

WiFi communication was selected for this project because it is directly supported by the ESP32 controller and allows simple implementation without requiring additional communication modules. It also supports real-time monitoring, remote data collection, and easy integration with IoT dashboards and cloud platforms.

Although ZigBee provides lower power consumption and better mesh networking capability, it requires additional hardware modules and increases implementation complexity. Since the proposed system operates inside a hospital building with existing WiFi infrastructure, WiFi communication is suitable for the prototype implementation.
