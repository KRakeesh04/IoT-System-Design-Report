\chapter{Method to Measure Water Level and Remaining Chemical Levels}

\section{Water Level Measurement and Chemical Estimation}

\subsection{Water Tank Measurement}

A non-contact ultrasonic sensor was selected for measuring the purified water tank level because the hospital requested that no sensing device should be installed inside the tank due to periodic cleaning and maintenance operations.

The ultrasonic sensor is mounted at the top of the tank and measures the distance between the sensor and the water surface. Since the sensor measures the empty space above the water, the actual water level can be determined by subtracting the measured distance from the effective internal height of the tank.

To improve measurement accuracy, the thickness of the tank lid and the wall thickness of the cylindrical tank are considered during calibration. The internal dimensions of the tank are used instead of external dimensions when calculating the actual water capacity.

Let:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $LT$ = Lid thickness
    \item $WT$ = Tank wall thickness
    \item $H_{ext}$ = External tank height
    \item $D_{ext}$ = External tank diameter
    \item $d$ = Measured distance from ultrasonic sensor to water surface
\end{itemize}

The effective internal tank dimensions are calculated as:

\begin{equation}
H_{int} = H_{ext} - LT - WT
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
r_{int} = \frac{D_{ext}}{2} - WT
\end{equation}

The total internal tank volume is therefore:

\begin{equation}
V_{tank} = \pi r_{int}^{2} H_{int}
\end{equation}

The water level inside the tank is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
Water\ Level = H_{int} - d
\end{equation}

The remaining water volume can then be estimated using:

\begin{equation}
V_{water} = \pi r_{int}^{2} (Water\ Level)
\end{equation}

The use of ultrasonic sensing provides several advantages:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Non-contact operation
    \item Hygienic measurement
    \item Reduced maintenance
    \item Protection against sensor damage during tank cleaning
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Chemical Estimation and Dispensing Control}

The system uses two separate liquid systems:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Concentrated chemical storage tank
    \item Diluted chemical dispensing tanks
\end{itemize}

According to the project constraints, only a low-level sensor can be installed in each dispensing tank. Therefore, a simple float-type low-level switch is used to detect when the dispensing tank requires refilling.

The concentrated chemical tank does not allow internal level sensors. Therefore, the remaining chemical quantity is estimated using a hybrid computational and external sensing approach.

Initially, the total volume of concentrated chemical inside the tank is known. During operation, the flow meter continuously measures the amount of chemical dispensed into the mixing chamber. The controller maintains a cumulative record of the total dispensed chemical volume.

The remaining chemical quantity is estimated using:

\begin{equation}
Remaining\ Chemical = Initial\ Volume - \sum Dispensed\ Volume
\end{equation}

To improve reliability and detect abnormalities such as leakage, an external load cell (weight sensor) can additionally be installed beneath the concentrated chemical container. The measured weight reduction can then be compared against the expected dispensed volume obtained from the flow meter.

This dual-verification method improves system reliability by enabling:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Leakage detection
    \item Flow meter fault detection
    \item Dry tank detection
    \item Abnormal dispensing detection
\end{itemize}

The dispensing process is controlled using a PWM-controlled peristaltic pump together with a flow meter. Although PWM adjusts the pump speed, it does not guarantee accurate liquid flow due to variations in pressure, viscosity, tubing resistance, and pump wear. Therefore, actual flow measurement using a flow sensor is essential for accurate dispensing control.

The refill operation stops automatically once the required target volume has been measured by the flow meter.

\section{Remaining Work}

The current implementation demonstrates the operational logic of the automated dispensing system using a single dispensing tank in a simulation environment. Several practical and industrial considerations remain for full-scale deployment.

\begin{itemize}

    \item The current prototype uses simulated flow pulses instead of a real industrial flow sensor.

    \item Communication between multiple dispensing tanks and the central controller was not fully implemented in the simulation and can be extended using MQTT, WiFi, or ZigBee communication protocols.

    \item The present simulation demonstrates only one dispensing tank. In a real hospital deployment, multiple dispensing tanks would be managed using a queue-based refill scheduling algorithm.

    \item The chemical concentration control was conceptually designed but not physically validated using real chemical mixing equipment.

    \item Cloud-based monitoring dashboards and mobile notifications can be integrated in future implementations.

    \item Additional industrial safety mechanisms such as redundant sensors, emergency shutoff systems, and battery backup systems may be incorporated for higher reliability.

\end{itemize}

Despite these limitations, the proposed system successfully demonstrates the core concepts of IoT-based automated chemical dispensing, including sensing, control, fault detection, and automated refill management.
